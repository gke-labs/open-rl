Reproducing LoRA Without Regret

Rank 1 against full fine-tuning on GSM8K RL, run concurrently on Open-RL · 2026-08-27

Thinking Machines report in LoRA Without Regret that for reinforcement learning, LoRA matches full fine-tuning even at rank 1. We ran that comparison at two model sizes, with every run training at the same time on one Kubernetes cluster. In the Qwen3-8B experiment, LoRA rank 1 finished 0.002 above full fine-tuning, against a spread of 0.016 between two runs differing only in seed. That is the paper's claim reproduced: at the lowest possible rank, the difference is smaller than the noise the measurement carries. LoRA rank 32 sat 0.023 above full fine-tuning, which does exceed that spread; the direction matters, since LoRA ahead of full fine-tuning is not a failure of the claim under test. Repeat seeds are what make these statements possible: they measure the noise floor rather than assuming it.

The claim

A policy gradient extracts on the order of one bit per episode. Supervised learning extracts O(tokens) per example, roughly a thousand times more per token. If that holds, an adapter with millions of parameters has enough capacity for an RL run carrying a few hundred thousand bits, and rank should not affect the outcome.

Method

3 configurations per experiment, each run 2 times under a different data-shuffling seed. Only the fine-tuning mode, the LoRA rank and the seed differ. Everything else is held fixed: dataset, group size, batch, token limit, temperature and step count. Learning rates keep the paper's ratio of about 10× between LoRA and full fine-tuning — equalising them would place each configuration at a different distance from its own optimum.

The runs execute at the same time rather than in sequence. They then share cluster state, dataset and GPU contention, which removes the drift between separate runs. The scheduler places LoRA runs sharing a base model on a single worker as separate adapters; full fine-tuning takes its own workers. Comparisons are made per step, not per second, since the shared runs progress at a fraction of the rate.

Pace

The endpoints agree, but the paths differ. Full fine-tuning reached 0.9 at step 19; LoRA rank 1 needed 27. Over the first 15 steps full fine-tuning averaged 0.48 against rank 1's 0.15. The paper's claim covers sample efficiency as well as final performance, so this is a difference worth naming. Two things plausibly account for it and neither is tested here: LoRA initialises B at zero, so its effective learning rate ramps up over the first steps, and we used a fixed 10× learning-rate ratio rather than sweeping each run to its own optimum, which is what the paper did.

What these runs do not show

Each step covers 64 episodes, so the Qwen3-0.6B runs saw about 2,560 and the Qwen3-8B runs saw about 2,560 each. The paper's MATH experiment used roughly 320,000. By its own information argument, capacity binds when episode-bits approach adapter parameters, and these runs are two to three orders of magnitude short of that. They show that rank 1 trains and keeps pace; they do not test the capacity limit.

Both models largely solve GSM8K at these settings — Qwen3-0.6B reaches 0.72, and Qwen3-8B reaches 0.96. Once every run is at its ceiling, agreement between runs is easy to obtain. A harder task is needed for a result that could fail.

Each configuration was run 2 times. The replicate spread bounds how much two runs differing only in seed disagree, which is what makes a gap interpretable; with 2 seeds it is a coarse estimate, not a confidence interval. The authors used Llama for GSM8K because Qwen's pretraining includes a large amount of mathematics; these runs used Qwen.

Infrastructure

Each run's trainer and sampler request a GPU through a Kubernetes ResourceClaim, and the claim's device selector decides placement. The tier comes from the model's parameter count: a 0.6B full fine-tune fits a 24 GB L4, while an 8B one needs an 80 GB H100, and an 8B LoRA worker needs one too, because the sampler must hold 16.4 GB of frozen weights inside the fraction of the device vLLM is given.

In practice, the Qwen3-0.6B experiment gave each of its 2 full fine-tuning runs a separate worker, and placed all 4 LoRA runs on one trainer and one sampler as 4 adapters; the Qwen3-8B experiment gave each of its 2 full fine-tuning runs a separate worker, and placed all 4 LoRA runs on one trainer and one sampler as 4 adapters. Packing adapters is the cost argument for LoRA in this setting — several adapters hold one copy of the frozen base weights rather than one copy each — and it is also why the LoRA runs took longer per step. The full fine-tuning workers were not dedicated either: replicates of one configuration were placed two to a claim, so the per-step figures below are not single-tenant numbers for any configuration.

Why full fine-tuning led early at Qwen3-8B

The endpoint tables say the configurations agree. The pace tables say full fine-tuning got there first at Qwen3-8B and not at Qwen3-0.6B. That asymmetry has a specific cause, and it is not capacity.

At Qwen3-8B, LoRA's reward goes negative around step 2 and stays near zero until roughly step 14 before recovering. Full fine-tuning never dips. Decomposing reward into its parts shows what happens.

The format score collapses for both LoRA ranks — from 0.17 to about 0.02 by step 2 — while full fine-tuning's climbs. Generation length explains it: the sampler is capped at 512 tokens, and the LoRA runs stay against that cap for a dozen steps while full fine-tuning's outputs shorten steadily from the start. Responses that run to the cap are truncated before they emit the answer in the expected form, so they score zero on format and on correctness.

The consequence compounds. With most completions failing, whole groups score identically, and a group whose rewards are constant carries no advantage signal and is filtered out. Around two thirds of groups are in that state for the LoRA runs through the early steps, against a quarter to a third for full fine-tuning. The LoRA runs were not learning more slowly from the same data; they were learning from a fraction of it.

This bounds what the sample-efficiency half of the comparison is worth. The paper's claim covers sample efficiency, and on these settings LoRA does reach a given reward later at Qwen3-8B. But the mechanism is an interaction between the learning rate, the token cap and early generation length — not the adapter's capacity to represent the update. A run with a larger token budget, or with each configuration swept to its own learning rate as the paper did, would be needed to separate the two. We did neither.

What full fine-tuning ships each step

Full fine-tuning keeps the trainer and the sampler in step by sending a sparse delta: the subset of parameters that changed, as values plus indices, rather than the whole tensor. LoRA has no equivalent, since it ships adapter weights that are small by construction. This section is therefore about the cost full fine-tuning pays to stay current, and it is the concrete form of the overhead LoRA avoids.

The saving is real but smaller than "sparse" suggests, because the payload is 6 bytes per changed element — a bf16 value and an int32 index — so indices cost twice what the values do. Halving the index width would cut a third off the transfer. These figures were checked against the safetensors files on disk rather than inferred from the element counts.

The shape is the same at both sizes: the first step rewrites more than a tenth of the model, and within five steps the update settles to two or three percent. The two replicates track each other closely, so this is a property of the training run rather than of a seed. Density never reaches zero, which is why every step pays a transfer.

  • Qwen3-0.6B: 0.36s to compute the delta, against 58s for the whole step — 0.6% of it.
  • Qwen3-8B: 1.65s to compute the delta, against 128s for the whole step — 1.3% of it.

Computing the delta is not the expensive part. The transfer and the sampler-side patch are, and they scale with density — so the first few steps, where density is highest, are also the slowest to synchronise.

Scheduling limitations this run exposed

The experiment was designed as a full crossing of model size, fine-tuning mode and replicate seed, and submitted as 12 concurrent jobs. Not all of them fit the available hardware. The ones that did not ran roughly twice as slowly as they needed to, and were still going long after the rest had finished. The reasons are properties of the placement layer rather than of the experiment, and they are worth separating from the scientific caveats above, because they would apply to any workload of this shape.

The cost of sharing a device is visible in the per-step times:

  • Qwen3-0.6B: 4 LoRA runs on one trainer and one sampler at 128s per step, against 58s for full fine-tuning — 2.2× for sharing.
  • Qwen3-8B: 4 LoRA runs on one trainer and one sampler at 286s per step, against 128s for full fine-tuning — 2.2× for sharing.

Sharing is not itself a defect — it is the reason LoRA is cheaper here, and under real contention it is the correct choice. What follows are three places where the system shares when it does not have to, listed in increasing order of what changing them involves.

1. Finished jobs keep their workers

Observed. Partway through, with the shorter runs complete, every worker Pod belonging to a finished run was still going, holding its ResourceClaim. Two H100 nodes and three L4 nodes were pinned by work that had already stopped.

Cause. The Kubernetes worker manager implements a per-model shutdown(), but the only caller is shutdown_all(), which is a no-op in that subclass. Nothing runs when a training job ends. The claim reconciler collects only claims with no referencing Pod, so a Pod that outlives its job keeps the claim alive indefinitely.

Change required. Call shutdown() when a worker's last active job finishes; the existing reconciler then reclaims the claim on its next pass. The one subtlety is that LoRA workers are multi-tenant, so this needs a per-worker count of live adapters rather than a per-job hook.

2. LoRA runs on one base model cannot spread across workers

Observed. The 4 LoRA runs on the larger model shared a single trainer Pod and a single sampler Pod, time-sliced 4 ways, while other GPUs of the same tier were free.

Cause. A worker Pod's name is derived from the base model with a fixed replica suffix, so every LoRA job on a given base model resolves to the same Pod. There is no way to ask for a second worker on that model. OPEN_RL_MAX_WORKERS_PER_CLAIM caps Pods per claim, which has no effect when the packing happens inside one Pod.

Change required. Thread a replica index through Pod naming and claim selection, and add a policy for how many adapters a worker takes before a new one opens. Packing is the right default under contention — N adapters on one device hold one copy of the frozen base weights instead of N — so the policy needs a capacity signal, not a fixed cap.

3. Placement is decided once and never revisited

Observed. As the full fine-tuning runs finished, their H100s went idle and stayed idle. The LoRA runs continued 4-way time-sliced next to free hardware for the rest of the experiment.

Cause. Claim selection runs once, at first launch. Base-model affinity is tried before any capacity test, and nothing re-evaluates the decision afterwards. A job that starts under contention stays packed even when the contention clears.

Change required. Two paths. The cheaper one is admission-time sequencing: hold jobs in a queue and launch as capacity frees, instead of starting every job at once. The complete one is migrating a live adapter — checkpoint, relaunch on the freed device, resume. The snapshot and restore machinery already exists; nothing drives it for rebalancing.

One theme connects them: the harness accepted a topology it could only serve badly and gave no indication that it had done so. Nothing reported that 4 of the 12 runs would land on one device, either when the jobs were admitted or while they ran. A placement summary at submission time would have surfaced this before the run rather than after it, and is smaller work than any of the three fixes above.
